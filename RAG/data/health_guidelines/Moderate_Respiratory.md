# Moderate AQI (101–200) — Respiratory

## Overview

When the Air Quality Index (AQI) enters the "Moderate" category (101–200), the air contains a noticeable concentration of pollutants that begins to have physiological impacts. According to the Central Pollution Control Board (CPCB) of India, this level can cause breathing discomfort, particularly to people with underlying lung diseases like asthma, as well as heart disease patients, children, and older adults. The World Health Organization (WHO) similarly notes that these elevated levels of particulate matter (PM2.5 and PM10) warrant caution, as the baseline threshold for safe exposure is being consistently exceeded. For the respiratory population, this tier serves as a clear warning to become mindful of environmental conditions. For individuals with asthma, COPD, or chronic bronchitis, a Moderate AQI is a direct threat. The CPCB clearly states this level causes breathing discomfort for lung disease patients, making proactive medication management essential. While a complete lockdown of daily life is not required, adopting specific behavioral modifications and timing outdoor activities carefully becomes an important health strategy.

## Health Effects

In a 101–200 AQI environment, the concentration of airborne toxins—such as fine particulate matter, nitrogen dioxide, and ground-level ozone—is dense enough to start overwhelming the body's primary respiratory defenses. These microscopic particles bypass the nasal hairs and mucus membranes, making their way into the deeper respiratory tract. The elevated particulate matter acts as a direct irritant to already inflamed airways, significantly increasing the risk of bronchospasms, excessive mucus production, and acute asthma attacks. The presence of these foreign particles triggers an immune response, leading to mild localized inflammation in the airways. While some individuals might only experience this as a slight irritation or increased mucus production, it serves as an indicator that the respiratory and cardiovascular systems are actively fighting environmental stress, which can lead to cumulative fatigue over the course of the day.

## Outdoor Activities

### Can I go for a morning walk?

Going for a morning walk in a Moderate AQI environment requires careful consideration of timing. During the early morning hours, temperature inversions frequently trap a concentrated layer of pollutants close to the ground, significantly worsening the localized air quality. Respiratory patients must avoid early morning walks due to the high concentration of ground-level smog. All outdoor walking should be delayed until the afternoon. If the air appears visibly hazy or carries a distinct smell, it is highly recommended to delay the walk until mid-morning or late afternoon, when rising temperatures and increased wind have helped to disperse the surface-level pollution.

### Can I go jogging or running?

Running and jogging demand a sustained, elevated respiratory rate, which inherently causes the lungs to pull in massive volumes of air. When the AQI is Moderate, this deep, rapid inhalation forces a larger quantity of fine particulate matter directly into the lower airways, entirely bypassing the nasal filtration system. Running outdoors is strongly discouraged for respiratory patients at this AQI level. The heavy breathing required will almost certainly trigger exercise-induced bronchoconstriction. While outright cancellation may not be necessary for everyone, it is advisable to reduce the intensity of the run, shorten the overall duration, or shift the workout to an indoor track if one is available.

### Can I cycle?

Cycling remains a viable option, but the environment heavily influences the risk level. Cyclists are often forced to share roadways with motor vehicles, placing them in direct, prolonged proximity to fresh exhaust fumes. In a Moderate AQI setting, this local traffic pollution compounds the already elevated regional background pollution. Cycling should be limited to very light, unhurried efforts in green spaces. Cycling in traffic pulls dangerous amounts of exhaust directly into sensitive lungs. Choosing routes through parks or quiet residential backstreets, and avoiding heavily congested commuting corridors, is essential to minimize the inhalation of localized, highly toxic exhaust spikes.

### Can I commute?

Outdoor commuting via walking, cycling, or public transport is acceptable, but proactive measures should be taken to minimize exposure. Commuting in closed vehicles with the air conditioning set to recirculate is the safest option. Avoid open-air transport like auto-rickshaws if possible. When traveling near busy roads, individuals are exposed to "hyper-local" pollution spikes that exceed the overall regional Moderate average. If commuting in a private vehicle, keeping windows rolled up and setting the air conditioning to recirculate indoor air is a prudent step to keep the cabin environment cleaner than the outside air.

### Can I exercise outdoors?

Structured outdoor exercises, sports, and intense physical training should be approached with caution. The increased metabolic demand of these activities amplifies the negative effects of the airborne pollutants. Intense outdoor exercise must be avoided. All aerobic training should be relocated to indoor gymnasiums to prevent triggering a severe respiratory event. Modifying the intensity of the workout, incorporating longer rest periods to allow the respiratory system to recover, and being willing to move the activity indoors if the air feels particularly heavy are vital strategies for safely exercising in this tier.

### Can I spend several hours outside?

Extended periods of outdoor exposure should be managed mindfully. While a Moderate AQI does not pose the severe, acute threat of higher categories, cumulative exposure over several hours slowly increases the total toxic load on the body. Respiratory patients should minimize prolonged time spent outdoors. They must always carry their prescribed rescue inhalers when leaving the house. The CPCB explicitly lists certain populations as vulnerable at this level; therefore, long outdoor picnics, extended yard work, or prolonged social gatherings should be broken up with indoor breaks in clean, well-ventilated environments.

## Should I Wear a Mask?

The necessity of a mask in a Moderate AQI depends heavily on individual vulnerability and the specific environment. While the general ambient air may not mandate universal masking, a high-quality N95 or KN95 respirator is highly recommended for sensitive individuals or anyone spending time near localized pollution sources, such as active construction sites or congested traffic intersections. An N95 or KN95 mask is strictly recommended for respiratory patients anytime they step outside, as it provides a critical physical barrier against the irritating particulate matter. Standard cloth masks and surgical masks are ineffective at filtering out PM2.5 and should not be relied upon for pollution protection; a proper seal against the face is strictly required for the mask to function.

## Indoor Recommendations

Even when the outdoor AQI is Moderate, maintaining good indoor air quality requires a balanced approach. It is generally advisable to keep windows closed during peak pollution hours—typically early morning and late evening—to prevent ambient smog from settling inside the home. Windows should remain closed during morning and evening hours. Running a HEPA air purifier continuously in the bedroom is highly recommended to ensure the lungs can fully recover overnight. However, short periods of ventilation during the relatively cleaner afternoon hours are still necessary to clear out indoor carbon dioxide and cooking fumes. Using an air purifier equipped with a HEPA filter in primary living spaces or bedrooms can provide a significant layer of protection and ensure the indoor air remains firmly in the "Good" category.

## Symptoms to Watch

Symptoms at this level are generally mild but serve as important warning signs of environmental irritation. Patients must monitor for increased wheezing, a tightening sensation in the chest, or the need to use their rescue inhaler more frequently than normal. Individuals may notice a dry or scratchy throat, intermittent coughing, mild eye irritation, or a slight feeling of heaviness in the chest. These symptoms indicate that the respiratory system is actively dealing with particulate matter. If these symptoms become persistent or begin to interfere with daily activities, it suggests that the individual's exposure limit has been reached, and they should immediately move to a cleaner indoor environment.

## When Should I Seek Medical Help?

While a Moderate AQI typically causes only discomfort, severe reactions require standard medical evaluation. If a respiratory patient uses their prescribed rescue inhaler and does not experience prompt relief, or if their breathing rapidly deteriorates at rest, they must seek immediate emergency medical care. If any individual—regardless of baseline health—experiences acute shortness of breath, persistent wheezing, sharp chest pain, or an inability to catch their breath even after resting indoors, they must seek immediate emergency medical care. These severe signs should never be dismissed simply as "pollution irritation" and require prompt clinical assessment.
